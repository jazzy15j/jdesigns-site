Why that cooking video made you so mad (and what it's actually doing)

You've seen it. Someone cuts an avocado the "wrong" way. Someone packs a meal prep container so full the lid can't possibly close. Someone cooks bacon in a pot of water like it's a soup. You didn't just scroll past it — you commented. That reaction wasn't an accident. It was the entire point of the video.

It has a name now

The tactic is called rage bait — content built to make you mad enough to comment, not content built to be useful. In late 2025, Oxford University Press named "rage bait" its Word of the Year, which tells you how mainstream this has become.

The formula is almost always the same: someone confidently does something that's obviously, provably wrong — cutting technique, overfilled containers, cutting pizza with scissors — and lets the comment section do the rest. A BBC cooking video of egg fried rice went viral for exactly this reason, racking up over 100,000 shares of the reaction to it. A TikTok of bacon cooked in a pot of water pulled in 9 million views and 25,000 comments. Almost none of that traffic came from people who thought, "great technique, I should try this." It came from people who couldn't let it go.

Why your brain falls for it every time

Here's the part that actually matters for anyone building content: being told something is wrong creates tension your brain wants to resolve immediately. You don't like sitting with "that's incorrect" — you want to either correct it or prove yourself right. Either way, you're typing.

That's not a food-content quirk. It's a hardwired response to a claim that contradicts what you believe. And it explains why "you're doing this wrong" is one of the most reliable hooks in existence — used honestly or not.

The part platforms actually punish (and the part they don't — yet)

This is where most people misunderstand what's happening. Meta officially penalizes engagement bait — but only the kind that explicitly asks for something: "Like if you agree," "Comment YES," "Tag a friend to win." Posts using that language get shown to fewer people, and repeat offenders lose more reach over time. That's documented, official Meta policy.

Rage bait never asks for anything. Nobody told you to comment on the avocado video — you just did. Because the reaction is technically organic, it currently slides past the same detection system. That's the loophole rage-bait creators are exploiting, and it's why it keeps working while "comment YES" posts quietly die in the feed.

It also explains why comments alone aren't even the biggest prize anymore. Instagram's 2026 ranking leans hardest on shares and saves — a rage-bait video gets forwarded ("you HAVE to see this") which is exactly the signal the algorithm rewards most.

What this means if you're not trying to go viral for the wrong reasons

You don't need to fake a mistake to use this. The mechanic — "you're doing this wrong, here's why" — works just as well when the wrongness is true.

The difference between rage bait and honest myth-busting is simple: one manufactures a lie to get a reaction, the other tells a real, defensible truth the audience needed to hear. Same tension. Same compulsion to engage. No trust burned when they find out you were right.

That's the version worth building a content strategy around: a real mistake your audience is actually making, stated plainly, followed by the fix. It gets the same comment thread, the same "wait, really?" pause — without torching your credibility the moment someone realizes the "mistake" was staged.

The takeaway

Rage bait works because being wrong is uncomfortable and correcting someone is satisfying. That's not a trick you need to fake — it's a pattern you can use honestly, every time you have a real answer to a real mistake your audience is making.

The creators cutting avocados wrong aren't smarter than you. They just found the one hook that always works. The good news: you don't have to lie to use it.
