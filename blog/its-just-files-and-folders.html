You don't need a smarter AI tool. You need a folder.

It has never been easier to open an AI chat and ask it something. It's never been harder to get that same chat to remember anything the next time you open it. The fix isn't a better AI. It's something most of us learned in ninth grade computer class and quietly forgot.

Make a folder.

Name it after your business.

2
Open ChatGPT or Claude.

That's it. No coding, no app to learn — just files and folders, the same thing you were taught in school, finally being used for something. Keep reading for the full walkthrough, and how I actually use this to run my own business.

The habit from freshman year computer class

Think back to that class. One of the first things anyone taught you was: save your file somewhere you can find it. Name it something that makes sense. Put it in a folder. Don't just dump it on the desktop. That was the whole lesson.

That one small habit — naming things, putting them somewhere on purpose — is what actually elevates how you use AI today. Instead of chatting with a tool and starting over from scratch every single time, you can ask a simple question and it pulls the answer straight from documents you already told it about you and your business. It never forgets. Not because the AI got smarter — because you gave it somewhere to remember things.

Why your AI keeps forgetting everything

Think about how you've probably used AI chatbots up until now — countless conversations about your business, your projects, your ideas. Good conversations. But you close the chat, or start a new one, and all of it is just gone. Nothing was actually being saved. Nothing was being done with it.

Here's the difference once you build real files: saved right on your desktop, linked to your cloud, backed up on GitHub, so you always have access to every one of them no matter what happens to any single chat. Every time you open an AI tool, it reads those files first, so it already knows what you told it to do and how you told it to do it — before you even ask the question.

There's a well-documented reason this habit needs saying out loud at all. Since 2021, computer science educators have been reporting — first in a widely-shared Stack Overflow podcast episode, then across multiple tech outlets — that a growing number of students never learned to think in folders and directories the way earlier generations did. Search replaced browsing, so "where did I save this" stopped being a question anyone had to answer. The habit didn't disappear because it stopped mattering. It disappeared because nothing forced anyone to use it — until now.

Two words, defined

A folder is exactly what it sounds like: a place on your computer where related things live together, like a drawer. I named mine "JDesigns" for my business. I've got a separate one for personal, and a couple others besides that.

A markdown file — you'll see it written as ".md" — is just a plain text file with a few simple symbols in it: a # for a heading, a * for a bullet point. On its own, it's not exactly pretty to look at. But that's not the point. You're not the one who has to read it. Open it in ChatGPT, Codex, Claude Code, or something like VS Code, and it gets understood instantly. Making sense of the symbols is the AI's job, not yours.

What "agentic AI" actually means

This category of tool has a name, and it's worth knowing: agentic AI. It's the difference between an assistant that only answers one prompt at a time, and one that can take a goal, break it into steps, use tools, and carry those steps out with minimal supervision. ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, and Copilot are all commonly described as agentic AI platforms as of 2026 — the ones that can actually read a folder full of files and act on it, not just chat about it.

How to actually start

If you're already paying for one of these tools, you probably already have what you need. A $20-a-month ChatGPT subscription includes Codex — the part of ChatGPT that can actually work with files and folders, not just chat back and forth. A $20-a-month Claude subscription includes both the regular chat and Claude Code, the version that does the folder work. Either way, you're not paying twice. You just have to go find it and click into it.

Here's the part that surprises people: you don't have to build everything from nothing. You can have it read the files already on your computer to start learning about you and your business before you organize a single new thing. Then make one folder that's just for this — just your AI stuff — and that's where the markdown files live from here on.

You don't have to figure out the whole system in one sitting, either. Go piece by piece. Have it ask you questions — what do you actually want out of this, how do you really run your business day to day — and let it build around your real workflow. You don't have to change how you already work. It adapts to you. It just also starts finding you ways to save money and time while it's at it.

What it looks like once it's built

Once it's set up, you're not the one keeping it organized anymore — it already is. What you're doing shifts. Less filing and remembering, more actually thinking.

Real example from my own business: someone messages me — a lead, a client, doesn't matter — and I paste it into my system. It already knows my prices, my rules for what I will and won't take on, how I actually talk to people. It classifies the message, flags anything that needs my attention, and hands me back a reply already in my voice. I still read it and decide if it's right before anything goes out — but the part that used to take the longest, the thinking part, is already done. I'm still the one deciding what actually goes out. The system just means I'm not starting from a blank page every single time someone messages me.

This isn't a fringe habit, either. In 2026 small-business AI adoption research, 58% of business owners already using AI tools this way reported saving more than 20 hours a month — time that mostly comes from exactly this: not re-explaining the same context over and over.

What this could do for your business

If you're a contractor: imagine every job in its own folder — before-and-after photos, the quote you gave, the warranty info, all in one place an AI tool could pull up instantly when a customer calls asking "what did we agree on again?"

If you run a small retail shop: your supplier order history, what actually sells each season, notes on what your regular customers tend to buy — organized once, so an AI tool can help you reorder smarter or plan what to stock next instead of guessing.

Whatever you run, the exercise is the same: name it, file it somewhere on purpose, and let an AI tool pick it up from there.

Stop renting the tools. Build them.

Once this is actually built, it keeps paying you back in a way people don't expect: you can build your own website, your own tools, your own apps with it — which means you start dropping subscriptions instead of collecting them. I built my own money management system, linked securely to my bank account, that reviews and categorizes every transaction automatically — the bank connection runs about $2 a month, and past that it's free. I built my own brand website, blog, and social content system that runs on the same AI subscription I already pay for, not a separate tool for each piece. I built the exact system I just described to you instead of paying for a CRM.

Once you can build it yourself, you stop renting it.
