Starting a store takes an afternoon. Running a profitable business doesn't.

It has never been easier to start a business — a print-on-demand store, a dropshipping shop, a service page. Starting got easy. Running a profitable business didn't. A lot of people are quietly assuming those are the same thing, and marketing usually gets blamed for the gap.

You can build a store this afternoon, upload a design to a print-on-demand platform, and be "selling" by dinner. You never touch the product. You never see a warehouse. A third party prints it, packs it, and ships it — you just picked the design and picked the platform. That's not a knock on it; it's genuinely one of the most accessible ways to start something that's ever existed. The print-on-demand market alone is worth over $13 billion in 2026, precisely because starting has gotten this easy.

The moment everything gets confused

This is usually the exact moment someone finds me. They've got the store. They've got a product, a logo, maybe even a few sales from friends and family. And now they need "marketing" — someone to set up their Facebook Page, get their Instagram looking right, launch an ad account, get some content out. That's real work, and it's work I do well, at prices anywhere from $245 for a setup to $1,200+ for a fuller strategy build.

But somewhere in that handoff, the expectation quietly shifts from "help me set up my marketing" to "make my business profitable." Those are not the same request, and no one making the first one should assume they're paying for the second.

What actually kills businesses (it's usually not this)

CB Insights studied why startups and small businesses actually fail. Marketing doesn't even crack the top five:

42%No market need — the product or service nobody was asking for
29%Ran out of cash — the most common failure point of all
23%Wrong team
20%Got outcompeted
18%Pricing or cost problems

The SBA's numbers back this up at scale: about 20% of businesses fail in year one, roughly 30% by year two, and by year five, close to half are gone. That's happening across every industry, every marketing budget, every ad platform — which tells you the failure point is rarely "we didn't post enough" or "the ad account wasn't set up right."

Marketing doesn't fix a business. It reveals one.

David Ogilvy, one of the most influential ad men who ever lived, put it plainly: "Great marketing only makes a bad product fail faster." Henry Ford said something similar almost a century earlier: "A market is never saturated with a good product, but it is very quickly saturated with a bad one."

That's not pessimism — it's just how marketing actually works. Good marketing gets more people to look at what you've built. If what they find is a strong product, fair pricing, reliable fulfillment, and good customer service, marketing accelerates that success. If what they find is a business that hasn't solved the fundamentals, marketing just gets more people to find that out faster.

Marketing doesn't build the foundation. It puts a spotlight on whatever foundation is already there.

What I'm actually selling (and what I'm not)

When you hire me for a Page setup, an ad account build, or a content package, here's exactly what you're getting: a clear, documented, professionally built piece of your marketing — the visibility and structure that gives your business a real shot at being found and considered.

Here's what you're not getting, because it isn't for sale from anyone, at any price: a guarantee that your product is right for the market, that your pricing covers your margins, that your fulfillment partner ships on time, that your customer service turns buyers into repeat customers, or that your cash flow survives a slow month. Those are the business. I'm the marketing. Marketing is one slice of running a profitable business — a real, necessary slice, but a slice.

The actual ask

If you're building a business right now — print-on-demand, dropshipping, or anything else where a third party is quietly doing most of the physical work for you — that's a legitimate way to start. But before marketing becomes the thing you're pinning your hopes on, get honest about the rest of it: Is there real demand for this? Do your margins survive your actual costs? Is your fulfillment reliable? Can you weather a slow month?

Hire a strategist to build the marketing piece right — that part matters, and it's worth doing well. Just don't hand someone a business that hasn't answered those other questions yet and expect the marketing to answer them for you. That's not what a digital strategist does. It's not what anyone can do.
