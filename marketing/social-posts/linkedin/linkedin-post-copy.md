## 2. Why Rage Bait Content Works

Graphic: `why-rage-bait-content-works.png`

```text
A video of someone cooking bacon in a pot of water pulled 9 million views and 25,000 comments.

Nobody watched it for the recipe.

Oxford named "rage bait" its 2025 Word of the Year for a reason: watching someone confidently do something obviously wrong creates tension your brain wants to resolve immediately. You don't sit with "that's incorrect" — you correct it, or you prove yourself right. Either way, you're typing.

Here's the part most people miss: Meta only penalizes engagement bait that explicitly asks for something — "comment YES," "tag a friend." Rage bait never asks. The reaction is technically organic, so it slides right past the same detection system.

You don't need to fake a mistake to use the mechanic. A real mistake your audience is actually making, stated plainly, followed by the fix, gets the same "wait, really?" pause — without torching your credibility the moment someone realizes nothing was staged.

What's the last piece of content that made you mad enough to comment? I'm curious how many of us fell for the same one.

Full breakdown: https://jdesigns.info/blog/why-rage-bait-content-works

#ContentStrategy #SocialMediaMarketing #ContentPsychology #JDesignsStrategist
```

## 3. Nike Doesn't Have 78,000 Marketers

Graphic: `nike-scale-small-business-expectations.png`

```text
Nike has 78,000 marketers. You've probably heard that one repeated as fact.

It's wrong. That's Nike's entire global workforce — warehouse staff, retail, logistics, everyone. Nike's actual marketing department, straight from their FY2026 SEC filing: 1,506 people.

Here's the number that actually matters. Nike spent $4.754 billion on marketing against $46.4 billion in revenue — roughly 10% of revenue. The U.S. Small Business Administration's long-standing guideline for any business under $5 million a year: 7 to 8%.

Read that again. The biggest athletic brand on the planet spends a percentage of revenue on marketing that's barely higher than what's recommended for a business a millionth its size. The gap isn't strategy. It's zeros.

If your business does $100K a year and you're putting 7-8% toward marketing — $7,000 to $8,000 — you're playing the exact same game Nike is playing. Not a smaller version of it. The same one.

What percentage of revenue are you actually putting toward marketing right now? Be honest — most people have never done this math.

Full breakdown: https://jdesigns.info/blog/nike-scale-small-business-expectations

#MarketingBudget #SmallBusinessMarketing #BusinessStrategy #JDesignsStrategist
```

## 4. Hiring a Strategist vs. Running a Business

Graphic: `hiring-a-strategist-vs-running-a-business.png`

```text
CB Insights studied why startups actually fail. Marketing doesn't even crack the top 5.

No market need (42%). Ran out of cash (29%). Wrong team (23%). Got outcompeted (20%). Pricing or cost problems (18%).

I say this as someone whose entire business is marketing strategy: hiring me does not fix any of those. David Ogilvy said it better than I can — "Great marketing only makes a bad product fail faster." Marketing doesn't build the foundation. It puts a spotlight on whatever foundation is already there.

When you hire a strategist, here's what you're actually getting: a clear, documented piece of your marketing — the visibility and structure that gives your business a real shot at being found and considered. What you're not getting, from anyone, at any price: a guarantee your product is right for the market, your pricing covers your margins, or your fulfillment is reliable. Those are the business. I'm the marketing.

If your last slow patch got blamed on marketing — was it actually marketing? Or one of the five above?
```

## 5. Big Brands Are Already Running AI Ads

Graphic: `big-brands-ai-ads-2026.png`

```text
Coca-Cola's AI-generated Christmas ad got called "soulless" two years running, with real boycott threats. Cadbury used AI to put Shah Rukh Khan's face on ads personalized for thousands of individual local shops — and nobody blinked.

Same technology. Opposite reception. The difference isn't the tool.

Coca-Cola and Toys "R" Us used AI to replace the human, emotional core people already loved about them. Cadbury and H&M used it to do something only AI could do at that scale — without touching the part of the brand people actually connect with.

Industry adoption of AI in ad creative is now somewhere between 67% and 90% among top advertisers, up from about 55% a year ago. This isn't a novelty anymore. It's the default.

You don't need a nine-figure budget to take the actual lesson from this: AI works when it's a tool applied with intention. It backfires the moment it's used to skip the thinking — or to replace the part of your brand that was never the problem.

Have you tested AI in your own ads or content yet? What worked, and what felt off?
```
